The small print

How this works

Two private lists, plus one shared. You each sweep your own life for the small things you want to keep up, and there's a third list for what belongs to both of you. Only your own list decides how your week went — so a busy month for one of you never turns into a competition.

Every item is daily, weekly, or monthly — and picking the right one matters most. Daily is for small, cheap things that lose their point if skipped: a walk, a call, lights out on time. Weekly is for anything that needs a proper slot: the reset, a date night, laundry. Monthly is for what you keep meaning to do and never schedule: the dentist, a subscription clean-up, a friend you haven't seen in a while.

Most failed attempts here aren't a willpower problem — they're the wrong item in the wrong place. A task that needs ninety minutes doesn't belong on the daily list. Put it there and it just gets missed, again and again, until the whole thing feels broken. It isn't. Move it to weekly or monthly instead.

Daily lists cap at five items each. Not a suggestion. Six items means negotiating with yourself every evening, and that negotiation is what kills it.

You get one flex day a week. Pick it at the weekly reset, before the week starts — never after a day you've already missed. It excuses every daily item except the ones you've marked as never-skip (pick at most two of those, or nothing feels special anymore). Days 29 and 30 don't get a flex day, so you finish clean.

Missing one day is fine. Missing two in a row is the thing to watch for. Habits usually die on the second missed day, not the first.

Only rearrange the plan at the weekly reset — never mid-week. Moving an item on a Sunday because it clearly doesn't fit is normal upkeep. Moving it on a Wednesday because you don't feel like it is how these things quietly fall apart.

Where to put things in a day

A daily item with no set time is just a wish. Give it one by attaching it to something that already happens on its own — coffee, the commute, closing the laptop, dinner, brushing your teeth.

  • Put your hardest item in your best hour, not whatever's left at the end of the day. Use the "best hour" you named in the reality questions.
  • Anything physical happens before dinner. After dinner, it usually doesn't happen at all.
  • Protect the twenty minutes after your day's hard stop — leaving work, closing the laptop, getting home. Those minutes decide what the rest of the evening looks like.
  • Put shared items early enough to actually happen. A call at 22:50 on the way to bed barely counts.
  • End the day at a fixed time. That's what makes tomorrow's list possible — and it's the step most people skip.

If one of you has a loose schedule — between jobs, on a break, working from home — the risk flips: nothing forces a start, so the day quietly slips. Fix a wake time and do the important thing early, before the day talks you out of it.

The weekly reset

Twenty minutes, same time every week — in person if you're together, a call if you're not. This is what keeps the whole thing running, so put it on the calendar like anything else. It's the first thing to disappear if you don't.

  • What got missed more than twice this week? That's a sign the item's in the wrong place, not that you're not trying. Move it or cut it.
  • What actually got done this week? Name the result, not the effort.
  • When will you see each other next week? Say the days out loud and put them in both calendars, especially if your weeks vary.
  • Which day is the flex day, and whose turn is it to plan anything shared?
  • One thing you'd like more of from each other.

That last question is the one couples skip, because it feels like inviting criticism. It's also the one that makes the other four worth doing.

What happens on day 31

Thirty days is enough to prove something works, but the ending is a cliff. Most of these fall apart the day after they finish, always for the same reason: nobody decided in advance what the smaller, permanent version looks like.

In week four, pick two or three items to keep for good. Not the whole list — just the ones that clearly paid off. Drop the rest, no guilt. A thirty-day plan that becomes three permanent habits is a win. One you try to keep in full is one you'll drop within a fortnight.

Check what's changing next. A new job, travel, a move — whatever you keep has to survive that, not just this month. If a habit only works in a quiet month, that's fine, but don't expect it to last past one.

How the two apps split the work

Todoist handles the day. Three projects: one each, kept private, plus a shared "Ours" project for the handful of things that are only weekly or monthly and genuinely joint. Even shared daily items appear on both of your own lists — Todoist can only mark a task done once, so a single shared task could never show that you both showed up. Ticking your own copy is what lets the tracker tell the difference. It notifies you, you tick it. That's the whole daily routine.

This form and the tracker handle the month. The plan, the targets, and the grid that shows whether you both showed up on the same day. Two private Todoist lists can't show that. The grid can.

Open this once a week, at the reset. Fill in the week, answer the four questions, pick next week's flex day. Twenty minutes. Not nightly — ticking the same item in two places is the fastest way to abandon both.

Nothing syncs backward. Finishing something in Todoist never updates this form. That's deliberate — two-way sync means conflicts and stored passwords and a system that breaks quietly. The plan only flows one way: decided here, done there, reviewed here.

One catch: Todoist's free plan only keeps a week of history. That's exactly a week, so don't run the reset late — run it eight days late and last week's completions are gone.
